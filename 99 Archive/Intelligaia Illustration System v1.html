01 · Foreword

Why this guide exists.

An illustration system is a contract. It says: here's how our pictures behave so our work feels like one voice, even when many hands draw it. Three principles set the contract.

Intent

Every illustration starts with a question — what should the viewer understand in two seconds? Tools are answers; questions come first.

Skip the question and you'll spend the rest of the week un-deciding.

Grammar

Repeatable rules outlast arbitrary taste. The system gives every contributor the same shelves, hinges and joints so the work compounds.

Personal style still lives — it just lives inside the grid.

Legibility

Agentic interfaces are read at glance-speed. If the picture takes ten seconds to parse, it's failed before the user has scrolled.

Simplicity isn't minimalism — it's respect for time.

Form fundamentals.

Before light, before character, before scene — the bones. How color reduces, how surface fills, how shape behaves, how depth is chosen, how the world is seen.

Contrast between the lightest and darkest shade should never collapse below 4.5:1.

Background tone is part of the palette — choose it before you start.

Neutrals (warm grays, sand, ink) carry the composition. Hues are the spice.

Surface mixtures are common — outline + solid + a single gradient is the workhorse.

Variable 1 — shape definition. Stroke weight defines the system's "voice."

Variable 2 — blending modes. Multiply over color for cheap, consistent depth.

Geometry mixtures are common — rigid frame, fluid character is a strong default.

Coherence across elements matters more than any single decision.

Set rules of behavior up-front. "Corners are always 4-radius" is a real rule.

Abstraction depth is not the same as level of detail. They scale independently.

The subject must remain recognizable. If you can't read it in 2 seconds, step back one level.

Abstraction is invention. It's the most editorial decision you make.

Perspective defines the type of spatiality — elevation reads as diagram, dimensional reads as object.

Complexity scales with visible faces. Two faces = friendly. Three faces = serious.

Variable — depth of field. A subtle background blur changes the temperature of the entire scene.

More detail does not mean more quality. Sometimes more detail is just more work.

Maintain consistent density across an illustration set or one piece will read as wrong.

Control the zoom. Picking density is picking how close the camera stands.

Pay attention to saturation. Accents at full strength fight the subject.

Accents give the system its personality. Use sparingly and they feel signature.

Try, save, retry. Build a small accent library before you commit.

Part Four

Applied foundations.

Once the bones are set, the body needs light, inhabitants and a stage. These three sections cover how illustrations behave in space and earn attention.

4.1

Define the direction of the light first — top-left at 30° is the safe default.

Pick one light model per set. Mixing makes the world feel like multiple worlds.

Shadows reinforce volume — they're not decoration, they're structure.

Characters direct attention. Wherever they look, the viewer looks too.

Define a body canon — proportions, head-to-body ratio, shoulder width, joint logic.

Attention to expressiveness and movement. Stiff figures kill an editorial system.

Pay attention to composition margins. Breathing room is a feature, not waste.

To create is to play. Set the rules so the game is interesting, not so the game is solved.

✱
Insight
It couldn't be missing — three thoughts to leave with.

Everything is a system.

Even a single illustration is a system of choices. Name the choices, repeat the choices.

Rules before pixels.

Spend the first hour deciding the grammar. The next hour will be three times faster for it.

Learn by looking.

The fastest way to get better is to study what's already good. Steal the principle, not the picture.
